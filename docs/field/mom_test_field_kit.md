# Arda Link — Field Kit: 10 Pastoralist Conversations (Mom Test)

*Get out of the building. Validate the problem before we polish the product.*

**Version 1.0 · Isiolo County · 10 pastoralist interviews + 5 ecosystem informants**

---

## 0. How to use this kit

This kit exists for one job: to find out whether the problem we think we are solving is
the problem a pastoralist in Isiolo actually has — in his words, with his numbers — and
to find out who the users, the beneficiaries and the payers are. Everything else
(polish, features, model tuning) is cheaper after this round than before it.

It is **not** a survey, not a sales visit, and not a demo. If we leave an interview
having explained our product and collected compliments, the interview failed. If we
leave with dates, amounts, distances, names and a story about a specific day last
season, it worked.

**What happens in this round**

| # | Step | Who | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Talk** (Mom Test, 40–60 min each) | 10 pastoralists, in their place | Their problem, their numbers, their words |
| 2 | **Watch** (usability, unaided) | the same 10 | Where the service loses them |
| 3 | **Ask for something** (commitment) | the same 10 | Callback, introduction, a test at the water point |
| 4 | **Ask the ecosystem** (20–30 min) | agrovet, county vet, chief, water committee, NDMA | Who decides, who pays, who can block |

**The one-page version of the whole kit**

| What we want to know | How we find out | Evidence that counts |
|---|---|---|
| Is the water/grazing decision frequent, hard and expensive? | Ask for the last three times it went wrong | A story with a place, a month, and a cost |
| Is information the binding constraint — not money, not water, not land? | Ask what he did, what he paid, how far he walked to find out | Money, hours, km, animals lost, litres of milk |
| Will he use WhatsApp and give one tap back? | Watch him use it, then ask for a commitment | He completes a task unaided; he reports once |
| Does our data survive his own knowledge? | Have him check our message, our map, our kg estimate | He confirms or corrects us, with his own number |
| Who benefits, and who pays? | Household questions + ecosystem informants | A real past payment, or a named budget line |

## 1. The rules of the conversation (Mom Test)

Read these in the vehicle. They are the difference between learning something and being
lied to politely.

1. **Talk about his life, not our idea.** Nobody tells you your baby is ugly —
   especially not a courteous host in his own manyatta.
2. **Ask about the past, never the future.** "Would you use this?" detects nothing.
   "When did you last have to decide where to move?" is data.
3. **Ask for stories, not opinions.** The moment he says "usually" or "I think", go back
   to "tell me about the last time".
4. **Numbers or it did not happen.** How many animals, how many days, how many
   shillings, how many kilometres, which month.
5. **Compliments are junk food.** "That is a good idea" means nothing. Thank him, and
   return to facts.
6. **Money questions are about the past.** Not "would you pay 50 bob?" but "when did you
   last pay money for advice or information — to whom, how much?"
7. **Never pitch.** If you are talking about our product for more than 30 seconds, you
   are not learning. One line, then straight back to his last season.
8. **Ask for something specific at the end.** Time (a callback), reputation (an
   introduction), a test. That is the only part of the conversation that is not free,
   and therefore the only part that proves anything.

**Three phrases that kill an interview**

| Do not say | Say instead |
|---|---|
| "Would you…" | "When did you last…" |
| "Do you think…" | "What did you do…" |
| "We are building…" | "Tell me more about that day…" |

**If he asks "what are you building?"** — one line, then back:

> "We are testing whether a phone can help with water and grazing decisions. But I would
> rather hear about your last dry season than talk about it. Can we go back to that?"

**Two of us at most:** one talks, one writes. The writer captures verbatim quotes,
numbers and place names — never interpretations. Paper, not a phone: a phone in your
hand reads as a distraction or a survey.

## 2. Who we are testing: users vs beneficiaries vs payers

Fill the empty cells as you go. The empty cells are the point of the round — most of these
are currently **our assumption**, not a fact.

**The model we are testing (state it before every interview, so nobody drifts):**

> The pastoralist is the **user and the beneficiary**, and he **never pays**. What he gives
> is different: **the data** — a one-tap water report, a pest observation, a herd change.
> What we sell is that stream, in aggregate, to the institutions that need ground truth
> and currently pay to collect it badly: the county, NDMA, NGOs and their M&E, insurers
> and research. So there are two sides to validate, and both can kill us: **will he
> supply?** (this round, the 10 interviews) and **will a buyer pay?** (the 5 ecosystem
> interviews). A service with no supply is empty; a stream with no buyer is a cost.

| Role | Who (Isiolo) | What they want | What it costs them today | How they relate to Arda Link | Gives / pays | Can block? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **User — decision maker** | Herd owner (usually an older man) | Not to lose animals; to know where to move | Wasted walks, dead animals, milk drop | Decides movement; may never hold the phone | **Gives:** a one-tap report when he is at the point | Yes — if he ignores it, it is dead |
| **User — the one who is there** | Young herder (moran/youth) with the animals daily | Water today, no wasted walking | Hours, km, heat | Highest one-tap compliance; reports status | **Gives:** the most reports, the most often | No |
| **User + primary beneficiary** | Woman in the manyatta (milk, water, shoats) | Milk, children's food, water, milk price | Walks to water, milk loss, sick shoats | Receives voice notes; reports water and queue | **Gives:** queue and shoats observations | Yes, quietly |
| **Beneficiary** | Household, children, elderly | Food, milk, income stability | Nutrition when herds fail | Indirectly | Nothing — that is why he never pays | No |
| **Gatekeeper + beneficiary** | Water point committee / WRUA | A working point, no fights at the trough | Queue conflict, breakdowns | Fans status out to members | **Gives:** confirms repairs, validates reports | Yes |
| **Channel + trust anchor** | Agrovet | Footfall, correct drug use | Wrong products bought, no follow-up | Distributes the number; answers health questions | **Gives:** distribution; may part-pay for footfall data | Yes |
| **Buyer — data + authority** | County livestock / water department | Surveillance, fewer outbreaks, service delivery | Collecting ward ground truth by vehicle and phone | Reads or buys the ward-level stream | **PAYS**, from a county budget line | Yes |
| **Buyer — data partner** | NDMA ward officer / drought monitoring | Timely ward ground truth on water and pasture | Fuel, staff time, late reports | Reads or buys the same stream | **PAYS** (monitoring / early-warning line) | Yes |
| **Buyer — M&E** | NGO / CBO projects, donor programmes | Reach, evidence, reporting to funders | Expensive last-mile data collection | White-labels the stream for M&E | **PAYS**, per ward per month | Yes |
| **Buyer — future** | Insurer / Takaful, research institute | Underwriting data; longitudinal herd data | Sample surveys, scarce baselines | Buys history once it exists | **PAYS**, later (needs 6+ months of records) | — |
| **Distribution** | Chief / assistant chief / ward admin | Order, service delivery, being seen to help | Nothing | Baraza announcement, signboard at the point | Nothing | Yes |
| **Us** | Arda Link | A service that survives contact with the field | — | — | — | — |

**The two questions this round must answer.** (1) **Supply:** will he contribute on a
rhythm — one tap, honestly, without being chased — because the value he gets back is
real to him? (2) **Demand:** is there a named institution with a real, current spend on
collecting this data, and a budget line we could sit in? If the round produces no supply
habit, there is no product; if it produces no buyer, there is no business — only a
service somebody else's grant pays for.

Pair this with the **decision-rights** question, because it decides the whole channel:

| Decision | Who actually decides | Who influences | Who must not be bypassed |
|---|---|---|---|
| When and where to move the herd | | | |
| Which animal to sell | | | |
| Whether to buy drugs, and which | | | |
| How much to spend on water or transport | | | |
| Who gets a phone / airtime / data | | | |

## 3. The sample: 10 pastoralists (plus 5 informants)

Ten is not a statistical sample. It is enough to see the repeated problem — and it is
deliberately skewed towards the people our design must not silently exclude.

**Screening (facts only, 3 minutes, before booking the interview)**

1. Do you keep livestock now? Which kind, and roughly how many?
2. When you last decided where to take them, who decided — you, or someone else?
3. Do you have a phone? Who uses it — you alone, or the household? Does it have
   WhatsApp?
4. How long does the walk to your usual water point take on the day you go?
5. In the last 12 months, did you lose animals, or have a bad tick or worm problem?

**Quotas for the 10** (adjust the numbers to the ward's reality, but keep the spread)

| Must include | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **3 women** (at least 2 keeping milking cows or shoats) | Beneficiaries are not the same as users; women's answers differ and are usually not asked |
| **3 farthest from water** (longest walk) | Where the problem is most expensive, and where our walk-distance claim is testable |
| **2 recently moved, or mid-move** | The decision is live — highest signal of the whole round |
| **2 camel keepers** | Different species, different movement logic, different water rules |
| **3 shoats-only or shoats-dominant** | The near-home herd: the woman's herd and the quick money |
| **4 cattle-dominant** | The classic case |
| **2 daily WhatsApp users** | Feasibility of the channel |
| **2 who never use WhatsApp** (voice or SMS only) | The users a channel-only design drops without noticing |
| **2 who share a phone with the household** | The "who actually receives the message" problem |
| **1–2 whose animal-health event went badly** | Tests the pest loop honestly, not optimistically |
| **≥2 wards, ≥3 different water points** | Stops one point's politics pretending to be a market |
| **not** our 3 existing registered herders | They already bought in; they cannot tell us about adoption |

**Ecosystem informants (5 × 20–30 min):** one agrovet, one county vet or sub-county
livestock officer, one chief/assistant chief, one water point committee member, one NDMA
ward officer or CBO project officer. Guides in Appendix D.

**Recruitment:** through the chief, the agrovet and the water committee — then
**snowball**: end every interview with *"who else should I talk to — and who would
disagree with you?"* The dissenter is worth three agreeable friends.

**Payment:** do **not** pay for an interview. Tea or a soda, yes. Transport
reimbursement only if he genuinely travelled to meet us. Never offer airtime for
*answers*: it buys polite answers, which are worse than no data.

Contribution is a different thing from an interview. He supplies the data that somebody
else buys, so the honest split is: **he is paid in value** (the water news, the tick
prompt, standing in the ward), and — only if the pilot shows the habit will not hold
without it — in airtime or data credit, documented as an incentive and never as a wage.
The herder never becomes our customer.

## 4. The interview: 8 questions, not 60

**Do not read the bank aloud.** An interview is eight story questions, in his language,
with silence in between. Everything else in this section is what you ask *while he is
telling you those stories*. A herder answering a list becomes a form-filler — and a form
fills with polite answers.

The eight, in the order they work. Each one is a story he can tell, not an opinion he can
agree with:

| # | Ask | It opens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tell me about yesterday — wake-up to bed | the division of labour: who holds the phone, who fetches water, who decides |
| 2 | The last time you moved the herd — which month, what made you decide? | the decision: who he asked, how long it took, what it cost |
| 3 | The last time you walked to water and it was not enough | the water failure: distance, hours, what he did next |
| 4 | When did the grass near your water last run out — and how did you know? | pasture: who saw it, what he tried, what he lost |
| 5 | The last time you found ticks or worms — what did you buy, at what price? | health spend, and who he trusts |
| 6 | Where do you get news of rain and grazing — and what has been wrong? | the incumbent sources, and their failures |
| 7 | Show me your phone: what do you do with a message you do not understand? | literacy, voice, network, charging, shared phones |
| 8 | If I asked you now, could you send one message about the water here? | the supply side: one tap, and how trust travels in the group |

Then the close (three asks), then twenty minutes alone with the notes. The one-page version
is `ArdaLink_Mom_Test_Questions.pdf` — that is what you carry.

**Everything below is the question bank:** the follow-ups, and the coverage list. Use it
*after* the conversation, to see which block the conversation did not reach and to record
what you heard (also in `ArdaLink_Mom_Test_Question_Bank.pdf`). The numbering is shared, so
a note tagged "D19" means the same thing in your notebook, in the bank, and in the tracker.

After each block there is a **Listen for** line — what counts as evidence — and an
**Avoid** line: the trap that fills notebooks with nothing.

### A. Warm-up: a normal day (5 min)

1. Tell me about yesterday — from when you woke up to when you slept. What did you do?
2. Who was with the animals yesterday? Where did they go?
3. Who else lives in your manyatta, and who does what in the day?

**Listen for:** the real division of labour — who is near the water, who holds the
phone, who milks, who decides. Most of our assumptions about "the user" die here.
**Avoid:** talking about "your herd" as if one person does everything.

### B. The last hard decision (10 min) — the core of the interview

4. Tell me about the last time you moved the animals to a new place. Which month was it?
   What made you decide?
5. Who did you talk to before you moved? What did each of them say?
6. How long did it take you to decide — from the first thought to the day you moved?
7. What was the first thing you tried, before moving?
8. What did the move cost you — money, animals, days, relationships?
9. Was there a moment when you almost went somewhere else? Why didn't you?

**Listen for:** a chronology with a month and a place name; the number of people
consulted (that is our real competition); the cost (that is the size of the problem).
**Avoid:** "Was that decision difficult?" He will agree with you. Ask what he did.

### C. Water: the last time it went wrong (10 min)

10. Tell me about the last time you walked to water and there was little or nothing.
    Which point, which month, how long did you walk?
11. What did you do that day? Did you go somewhere else — how far?
12. What did that day cost you: hours, money, fuel, animals, tempers?
13. Is there a point you stopped going to? Why?
14. Who tells you whether a point is working, or has a long queue? How do you find out?
15. Have you ever paid someone for information about water? What happened?

**Listen for:** a specific day, a distance, a duration, a queue, a breakdown, and a
workaround (phoning someone, sending a boy ahead, waiting, going anyway). Every
workaround is evidence that the problem is real and currently unsolved.

### D. Grazing and pasture (10 min)

16. When did the grass near your water last run out? Which month?
17. How did you know it was finished — who saw it, and what did they see?
18. What did you do next? Who suggested it? Did it work?
19. How many animals did you lose, or how much milk did you lose, in that period?
20. Does anyone tell you in advance where the grass is still good? Who, and how often?
21. Have you ever paid for news about pasture — a phone call, transport to go and look,
    a gift to someone?

**Listen for:** the sequence *grass finished → I asked X → I went → it was good/bad*.
That sequence **is** our product today, done by word of mouth and by walking.

### E. Animal health, ticks and worms (8 min)

22. Tell me about the last time you found ticks or worms in your animals. Which month?
23. What did you do first? Who did you ask?
24. What did you buy, from whom, and how much did it cost? Did it work?
25. Who told you to look at the animals — or did you just see it yourself?
26. If you had been told a week earlier that tick conditions were forming, what would you
    have done differently? Be specific: what did you do last time?

**Listen for:** whether he *looks* on a rhythm or only when something is visibly wrong —
that is precisely the gap the check-first loop fills — and the money spent on drugs,
which is the loss we would be reducing.
**Avoid:** any animal-health advice. We do not diagnose in the field; that is the vet's
and the agrovet's role. If he asks, refer him, and note that he asked.

### F. Where information actually comes from today (8 min)

27. Where do you get news about rain and grazing? Radio, chief, neighbours, the clinic,
    a WhatsApp group, the vet? Which one first?
28. Which of those has ever been wrong in a way that cost you something? What happened?
29. What did you believe last week that turned out to be wrong? How did you find out?
30. How much do you spend in a normal month on airtime and data? What do you use it for?
31. Are you in a WhatsApp group with other herders? How many? What is it called? Who
    forwards to whom? (If he offers to show you, look.)

**Listen for:** the incumbent (chief, radio, neighbour), the cost of finding out, and the
forwarding graph. That graph is our distribution channel and our acquisition cost.

### G. The phone in his hands (5 min)

32. Whose phone is this? How many SIM cards? Who else uses it?
33. When did you last have no network where you keep animals? How long did it last?
34. How do you charge it? What happens when the battery dies?
35. Can you read comfortably on it? Do you read messages, or do you play voice notes?
36. Show me what you do when you get a message you do not understand. (Watch. Do not
    help.)

**Listen for:** shared phones, dead spots, charging, and voice-versus-text. If he only
plays voice notes, every text-only feature we ship is invisible to him.

### H. Money (8 min) — past spending only

37. What did you spend on your animals in the last month — drugs, water, transport,
    feed, a vet? Roughly how much of that was cash?
38. When you need 5,000 shillings quickly, what do you sell, or who do you call?
39. Have you ever subscribed to something on your phone — a data bundle, an SMS service,
    betting? How much per month?
40. Have you ever paid for advice — a vet, an agrovet, a broker, a transporter? When, how
    much, to whom — and was it worth it?

**Listen for:** real amounts for real things — drugs, transport, airtime, water fees. Note
what this is NOT: it is not willingness-to-pay for our service, because **the herder is not
the buyer** — the people who pay are the institutions that use the data he supplies
(county, NDMA, NGO, insurer — see section 2). What it tells us is the size of the loss we
are reducing, and the phone and airtime he already carries.

### I. Trust, blame and failure (5 min)

41. Tell me about a time when advice about your animals or the rain turned out to be
    wrong. What did you do about it?
42. If a phone told you the rain would come in five days and it did not come, what would
    you do with that phone?
43. Who would you believe about rain: the chief, the radio, the vet, a neighbour, or a
    message on your phone? Why?
44. What would make you stop trusting a service like this completely?
45. If a service said "we do not know" about something, is that better or worse than a
    guess?

**Listen for:** his tolerance for uncertainty, and the named authority we are compared
to. Question 45 is the honesty test — the whole design depends on the answer.

### J. Have others tried? (3 min)

46. Which organisations have given you information or services like this before — an NGO,
    the county, radio, church, insurance? What happened? Is it still running?
47. Why do you think it stopped — or what would have made you keep using it?

**Listen for:** the graveyard of previous attempts, and why they died. Usually: no
rhythm, no reply, no trust, or the project ended.

### K. Household and beneficiary — ask the women separately (5 min)

48. When you get news early — about water, grass or rain — who in the household benefits
    first, and what do they get?
49. Who fetches water and waters the small stock? How does the walking change with the
    seasons?
50. Who decides what happens to the small stock and the milk money?
51. What would change in your house if you never wasted a single walk for water this
    year? What would that be worth to you?

**Listen for:** the beneficiary chain — milk, children, women's time — and whether the
value lands where we claim it does.

### L. Contributing information (5 min) — the side that makes the data sellable

We are not testing whether he will pay. We are testing whether he will **supply** — because
the supply is the product, and somebody else buys it.

52. Have you ever told anyone that a water point was broken, or working? How did you do it
    — a call, the group, in person?
53. The last time you were at the water, could you have sent one message if you had been
    asked? Did you?
54. When you pass information to a group, how do you make people believe it — a photo, a
    voice note, your name? Who do they believe?
55. What would make you stop contributing information?

**Listen for:** whether he already reports by habit (that is our head start), how trust is
established in the group, and what the honest cost of one tap is to him. Unreliable supply
kills the data product before any buyer sees it.

### M. Close: ask for something (5 min)

56. May I come back or call you in two weeks with what we learned? *(time)*
57. Would you show me your phone and let me send one message to this service from your
    number, now, while I watch? *(micro-commitment + an unaided attempt)*
58. Who else should I talk to — and who would disagree with you? *(introductions)*
59. Could we do a test at your water point with the committee or the group?
    *(advancement)*
60. What did I get wrong today? What should I have asked you and did not?

**Listen for:** a yes with a constraint is better than an enthusiastic "yes, definitely"
with nothing attached. No introduction, no callback, no test = no signal.

## 5. Watch, do not talk: the product tests during the visit

Talking gives us the problem. **Watching gives us the product.** Run these on the live
service — on his phone where possible — with no coaching. Record the outcome and one
verbatim reaction for each.

| # | Test | How to run it | Record | What failure would mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | **Unaided find** | Hand him the phone: "find out whether the water at your point is working." Nothing else. 5 min max | Completed / partial / failed; seconds; where he went wrong; voice or text | The menu and wording lose the least literate users first |
| T2 | **Comprehension** | Show the real messages (water line, pest window, MALISHO YA LEO, weekly note): "what does this say? what would you do?" | Items he misreads, in his own words; numbers he repeats wrongly | Our careful wording fails in the field even when it passes our tests |
| T3 | **One tap back** | Ask him to report the status of the point he is standing at, now | Did he complete it; did he refuse; did he ask what he gains | The reciprocity loop is the engine — if he will not tap, re-plan |
| T4 | **Voice vs text** | Send the same content both ways; ask which he would rather get at 6am; note which he opens first | His choice and reason; whether the voice note plays at all on his phone | A voice-first user base served text-only |

| # | Test | How to run it | Record | What failure would mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T5 | **The map** | Show the rendered map/paper copy: can he find his manyatta, the point, the direction? What do the date stamp and the word "kadirio" mean to him? | Whether the place names match what he calls them; whether he trusts the age stamp | Our riskiest artefact: a map that misleads is worse than no map |
| T6 | **The kg question** | Does he own, borrow, or know a scale (market, vet)? What real weight does he know for one animal? Would he walk to a scale day? What would he use a weight for? | A reference weight we can check against; his own words for what a weight is worth | Without a reference weight, the weight output stays shadow-only |
| T7 | **Uncertainty tolerance** | "If we say rain in five days and it does not come — what then?" | Whether honest uncertainty costs trust or buys it | Decides whether "makadirio" wording stays |
| T8 | **Forwarding** | "If you got this, who would you send it to?" | The forward path, group names, who the hub person is | Distribution and acquisition cost |

**Rules for watching:** do not touch the phone, do not explain the screen, do not rescue
him, never ask "is it easy?" When he gets stuck, write down exactly where, and only then
help.

## 6. Notes: what counts as evidence (write this way)

One writer, one notebook, and one discipline: **verbatim, in his language, with
numbers.** Never your summary. If you are writing "he was happy with the idea", you have
written nothing.

Tag every note as you write it, so the analysis is mechanical:

| Tag | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `FACT` | what he did, where, when | "Walked to Kipsing, 2.5 hrs, pump was broken" |
| `NUMBER` | money, distance, time, animals, litres | "Paid 3,500 for acaricide; lost 4 shoats" |
| `QUOTE` | his exact words, translated literally | "Nilipoteza siku nzima, mifugo ilikauka" |
| `WORKAROUND` | what he does instead of a service | "Sends a boy ahead to look before moving the herd" |
| `PAYMENT` | anything he has actually paid for | "50/= a month on data, 200/= to the vet for a visit" |
| `TRUST-BREAK` | a time advice failed him | "The radio said rain; we moved and it did not come" |
| `COMMITMENT` | something he agreed to do, with a date | "Will bring 5 neighbours on Saturday" |
| `BLOCKER` | what stops him acting on advice | "No money for drugs even when told early" |

**After every interview, before the next one (20 minutes, same day):**

1. Write the **three-sentence story**: who he is, the one problem that mattered most, and
   what he does about it today.
2. List the numbers you got. Fewer than three numbers means the interview was too polite.
3. Mark each hypothesis in section 7 `SUPPORTED / CONTRADICTED / UNKNOWN`, with the quote
   or number that decided it.
4. Write down one thing we should **stop** believing.

## 7. The hypotheses, and what would prove us wrong

Each row is falsifiable. The right-hand column is the point: decide **in advance** what
would change the product, so the decision is not made by the person who built it.

### Desirability — is this the problem?

| ID | Hypothesis | Question that tests it | TRUE if | FALSE → kill/pivot if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | The water and grazing decision is frequent, hard and expensive | B4–B9, C10–C15, D16–D21 | ≥6/10 describe a specific episode with a month, a place and a cost | <4/10 can describe a specific loss → the problem is real but not acute enough to change behaviour |
| D2 | Information is the binding constraint — not money, water or land | C11–C12, E23–E26, H38 | They pay or travel to find out, and act on what they learn | They know and still cannot act → we are optimising the wrong constraint |
| D3 | The loss is big enough to matter to a household | D19, H37, K52 | Named losses in animals, milk or shillings in the last 12 months | Losses are rare and small → the willingness to change behaviour will be small too |
| D4 | It is a recurring decision, not once a year | B4, C10, D16 | Weekly-to-monthly cadence in ≥6/10 | Once a season → a weekly rhythm is the wrong product |

### Usability — can he use it, unaided?

| ID | Hypothesis | Question that tests it | TRUE if | FALSE → kill/pivot if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| U1 | A herder can find and use a WhatsApp service without training | T1, T3, G36 | ≥7/10 complete the task unaided | ≤4/10 → complexity, not content, is the product risk |
| U2 | One tap is a price he will pay to receive something | T3, L54 | ≥6/10 report once, or agree to a test | Most refuse → redesign the reciprocity loop, or drop it |
| U3 | Voice beats text for the people who matter most | T4, G35 | Voice is chosen by the least literate and the most remote | Text dominates → re-allocate our effort |
| U4 | Our messages are understood the first time | T2 | ≥8/10 restate the advice correctly in their own words | Common misreadings → the wording is wrong even when the logic is right |

### Feasibility — can we stand behind it?

| ID | Hypothesis | Question that tests it | TRUE if | FALSE → kill/pivot if |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## 8. Turning 10 conversations into a decision

### Same day, 20 minutes (do not skip this)

Write the three-sentence story, list the numbers, mark the hypotheses, note one thing to
stop believing. Interviews blur together within a day — and the ones you did not debrief
are the ones you will mis-remember in your own favour.

### Day 6–7: cluster, then count

Build this table from the notes. One row per problem, sorted by how many mentioned it
**unprompted** — unprompted mentions are the only ones that count.

| Problem (in their words) | Unprompted /10 | What it costs today (their numbers) | What they do now | Does our product address it? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | |

Then write, one sentence each:

1. **The problem statement, in their words.** Not ours. If it needs three sentences, it
   is three problems.
2. **Who is who** — section 2 completed, with real names and real roles.
3. **What we must not build** — the features this round would only have justified if the
   interviews had gone a different way.
4. **The one number that would change our mind** — written down before the next round.

### The decision gate

| Result | Condition | What we do next |
|---|---|---|
| **GO** | ≥6/10 describe a specific, recurring, costly water or grazing decision **and** ≥6/10 either already pass news on or complete a report in front of us (supply) **and** ≥1 named institutional buyer with a real current spend on collecting this data **and** a named budget owner **and** ≥4/10 accept a commitment | Keep the same loops; move to a 6-week pilot with 30 households in one ward, and watch two numbers: reports per point per week, and whether the buyer's conversation reaches a budget line |
| **PIVOT — supply** | The problem and the buyer are real, but most pastoralists will not report on a rhythm without being paid | Change the loop: report only when something is wrong, piggyback on the agrovet's visit, or pay in airtime/data and measure whether the habit then holds |
| **PIVOT — channel** | The problem is real, but the decision maker has no phone, or the phone holder does not decide | Change the entry point: voice notes to the phone holder, a signboard at the point, the agrovet as the front door, or the water committee as the account holder |
| **PIVOT — problem** | The pain is animal health, market price or water infrastructure, not grazing decisions | Re-plan around what they named: keep the platform, change the promise |
| **PIVOT — segment** | The pain is concentrated: only the far, large-herd households have it | Narrow the segment on purpose; a broad launch spends money on the wrong people |
| **REFRAME / STOP** | <4/10 can describe any specific loss with numbers in the last 12 months | Stop building features. Test the problem again with a different segment, or a different problem |

### Seven ways to fool ourselves when analysing

1. **Counting prompted agreement as a mention.** "Yes, that happens too, sometimes" is
   not a mention.
2. **Averaging opinions.** Ten opinions are worthless; ten stories with numbers are data.
3. **Over-weighting the articulate.** The fluent English speaker is usually the most
   powerful herder in the ward, not the median user.
4. **Ignoring the woman's separate answer.** If she was not interviewed alone, you do not
   have her answer.
5. **Letting the chief choose the sample.** He will pick people like him.
6. **Treating our own registered users as market signal.** They already said yes.
7. **Falling in love with the demo.** A smooth demo in a manyatta proves only that we can
   drive our own product.

**Two rules for reading this table**

- Read every row twice. Once as **his** question: does this make his day better, so he keeps
  using it? Once as the **buyer's**: does this make the data stream better, so somebody pays
  for it? A feature that only answers the first is a cost. A feature that only answers the
  second is a survey, and he will stop opening it.
- A feature whose evidence column is still empty after ten interviews is a liability: it
  costs attention and buys nothing.
- A feature whose evidence is **contradicted** is not a failed round. It is the round
  doing its job.

## 10. Ethics, consent and data protection (Kenya DPA 2019)

We are asking people to give us their time and their knowledge, in a context where
"NGO" has a history. These rules are not decoration.

**Ask before you start, out loud, in his language (full script in Appendix A):**

- Who we are and what we are doing (research, not the county, not a relief programme).
- That this is voluntary, that he can stop or skip anything, and that saying no has no
  consequence for him with anyone.
- That we want to write down and record what he says, that we will use it to decide what
  to build, and that we will not use his name in anything we publish without asking.
- That there is **no payment** for this today, and that we may not build anything at all.
- Then: "May we start? Are you comfortable with the recorder on?"

**What we never do in the field**

| Never | Because |
|---|---|
| Give animal-health advice, a diagnosis, or a drug name | That is the vet's and the agrovet's role; we are not there to practise |
| Promise water projects, jobs, handouts, cash, airtime, or that the service will continue | Creates expectations we cannot meet and biases every later answer |
| Quote him by name without explicit permission | Trust and, under the DPA, personal data |
| Photograph him, his home, his animals or his documents without a clear yes | Private space, and no photo is worth losing the relationship |
| Interview children, or ask about conflict, land or ethnic politics | Out of scope and unsafe |
| Discuss his household money in front of others if it visibly causes friction | The woman's answer must not cost her anything |

**If he asks for something we cannot give** (a pump repair, drugs, a lift):

> "I cannot fix that, and I am not the county. What I can do is write down exactly what
> you told me, and if you want, I can tell you who to call. Should we do that?"

Then note it as `BLOCKER` evidence. Those asks are the most valuable thing in the
interview — they show the real gap.

**Data handling**

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Consent | Verbal, recorded or noted, with the date and the person's initials |
| Names | Initials or a number in the tracker; full names only in the paper notebook, stored with the kit, not on a shared drive |
| Recordings | Kept locally, deleted after transcription (target: 30 days) |
| Transcription | Verbatim quotes only; the audio is not the deliverable |
| Sharing | Findings shared anonymised; no quotes with a name unless permitted |
| His rights | Tell him he can ask us to delete what he said, and honour it |
| Sensitive data | None: no health data about him, no financial account details, no ID numbers |

## 12. Pre-mortem: how this round could still be a waste of time

Answer these before the fieldwork, then check them afterwards.

| Way it fails | The early signal | What we would do |
|---|---|---|
| We interview the wrong people (the chief's friends, the fluent English speakers) | The sample is all men, all literate, all WhatsApp users | Re-run with the quotas enforced: the missing voices carry the answers |
| We get polite agreement instead of pain | Notes are full of "they liked it", with almost no numbers | Change the script: show nothing for the first 25 minutes |
| The problem is real but he cannot act on it | He says he already knew, and did nothing | Our constraint theory is wrong: look at money, mobility, or the collective decision |
| The phone is not his at the moment of decision | The phone is shared, or the animals are out with someone else | Redesign around the real decision moment and the real phone holder |
| Our data cannot bear the claim at the decision moment | He corrects the map, the point status, the kg estimate | Reduce the claim, show the age, or drop the artefact |
| No buyer exists | No budget line surfaces anywhere, and nobody can name a current spend on collecting this data | Keep running on grants deliberately, and hunt the buyer before adding features — or accept that this is a public good and say so |
| The supply decays | Reports arrive for a week and then stop; nobody reports without being chased | The value he receives is not enough to pay for his one tap: fix the value, or compensate, and re-measure |
| A gatekeeper blocks us (politics, county turf, both-herd disputes) | Someone asks why they were not consulted first, or claims the data | Negotiate formally — chief, ward, county — in writing |
| We learn a lot and change nothing | No row in the decisions log with an owner and a date | The round has failed: the log entry is the deliverable, not the notebook |

## Appendix C — Ecosystem informant guides (5 × 20–30 minutes)

These are not users. They tell us who pays, who blocks, who carries it, and what is
already being paid for.

### C1. Agrovet (the counter, the cash and the trust)

1. What do herders come in and ask about most — and what did they ask yesterday?
2. Which products do they buy for ticks and worms, and what do those cost?
3. When a herder cannot pay, what happens? Do you keep a record, or credit?
4. Who tells them to come — a vet, the chief, a neighbour, or nobody?
5. How would customers hear about a phone service with early tick warnings: could it be
   printed here, or mentioned at the counter? (Past tense: has he ever carried anything
   like this before, and what happened?)
6. How many herders do you see in a week, and do they come on a rhythm?

### C2. County vet / sub-county livestock officer (authority, content, and likely buyer)

1. What surveillance do you have, and where does it come from today?
2. What did you spend last month collecting data from the wards — fuel, staff time,
   airtime, surveys — and who did that money go to?
3. Have you ever bought data or a service like this: a survey firm, a CBO, a partner
   project? What did the contract cost, and which line paid it?
4. Which budget line would a ward-level water-and-pasture stream come from, who signs it
   off, and when does that cycle close?
5. If such a stream arrived weekly, what would it replace — and what would you stop paying
   for?
6. Whose wording do you want used for tick/worm messaging — and who signs it off?
7. What would make the county object to this service operating in the ward?

### C3. Chief / assistant chief (the gate)

1. How do you announce something to the ward, and when is the next baraza?
2. Who would you ask to test something like this — and who would you leave out? (That
   answer is his sampling bias: write it down.)
3. What has an NGO or the county promised this ward before, and what happened?
4. Would a signboard with a number at the water point work? Who maintains it?
5. What would make people distrust a service like this?

### C4. Water point committee / WRUA (the operators)

1. How do you learn that the pump is down, and how do you tell people today?
2. How many people use this point, and does the queue have a pattern by hour or day?
3. What do users contribute — cash, labour, livestock? How much, and when?
4. If the phone service reported a breakdown, what would you do differently?
5. Would the committee host a test here — and who has to agree?

### C5. NDMA ward officer / CBO project officer (data and money)

1. What ward-level data do you hold, how is it collected, and how old is it by the time you
   use it?
2. What did the last collection round cost, and which line paid for it?
3. Have you ever bought a data stream or a monitoring service? From whom, how much, and
   what did you have to do to get it procured?
4. What would you need to see — and who would you need to convince — before putting this in
   a budget or a proposal?
5. Who else in the county or the NGO world buys this kind of data, and what do they pay?
6. Who else in the ward should we be talking to — and who would disagree with this?

## Appendix D — Follow-up call, two weeks later (15 minutes)

The cheapest retention test we own. Call the ones who accepted.

1. Since we spoke, have you had to decide where to move, or where to water? What did you do?
2. Did anything we showed you change what you did? What, exactly?
3. Did you tell anyone about it? Who?
4. Did you look at the message again? How many times?
5. Would you still take my call in another two weeks?

**Interpretation:** something he did *without being asked* is the strongest signal available
outside a pilot. "I forgot" is the honest answer and the most useful one — it means our
service is not yet part of his rhythm.

## Appendix E — The tracker spreadsheet, and how this kit is generated

`ArdaLink_Mom_Test_Tracker.xlsx` ships with this kit and is where every interview becomes
a row. Sheets:

| Sheet | What you do with it |
|---|---|
| `01_Screener` | Book interviews: the five screening facts, which quota each one fills, and whether the sample still has gaps |
| `02_Hypotheses` | Every hypothesis with its falsification rule; score each interview `Y / N / ?` and record the evidence id |
| `03_Interview_Log` | Date, place, duration, consent, recording, who was present, product-test outcomes |
| `04_Quotes` | Verbatim quotes, tagged (`FACT`, `NUMBER`, `QUOTE`, `WORKAROUND`, `PAYMENT`, `TRUST-BREAK`, `COMMITMENT`, `BLOCKER`) with the section letter |
| `05_Numbers` | Every number he gave: item, value, unit, and which question it came from |
| `06_WhoIsWho` | The user / beneficiary / payer / gatekeeper map, completed with real names and roles |
| `07_Gate` | Automatic totals: mentions per problem, hypothesis support, and the GO / PIVOT / STOP verdict |

The same content also lives as markdown in the repository
(`docs/field/mom_test_field_kit.md`), which is the source of truth for the `.docx` and the
`.pdf`. Edit the markdown, re-run the generator, and every format updates together — so the
questions asked in the field can never quietly drift from the questions we agreed on.

**Before the first interview, three things**

1. Fill the sample quotas in `01_Screener` with names from the chief, the agrovet and the
   water committee.
2. Print the pack: crib sheet, consent cards, 15 interview sheets, the Swahili guide and
   the five informant guides.
3. Send one real message from your own phone into the live service. The number, the message
   and the flow you intend to show must be tested *before* you are sitting in front of a
   herder with one bar of network.
